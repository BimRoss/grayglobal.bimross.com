Cost & delivery · Infrastructure

Reading cost escalation early

By David Glossop · Gray Global

Major infrastructure budgets rarely move by surprise. A road programme that doubles over six years, a rail tunnel that climbs steadily through delivery, an LNG build that lands well above its early figure, these are not random shocks. The pressure was usually visible in the business case, for anyone reading it to find the escalation rather than to confirm the number.

The difficulty is that the early estimate does two jobs at once. It is meant to be a forecast of cost. It is also, in practice, the figure that secures approval and funding. Those two jobs pull in opposite directions, and on a programme with momentum behind it, the approval job usually wins. The honest forecast arrives later, in change orders.

A low number that wins approval and a real number that survives delivery are not the same number. Know which one you are holding.

Five questions worth asking before close

What is the estimate anchored to? An estimate built up from comparable delivered projects is a different animal from one built down to a target. Ask what the base is, how old the reference data is, and whether the comparables actually resemble this project in ground, scale and location.

How is contingency sized, and who can spend it? Contingency set to make the headline work is not contingency, it is a gap waiting to open. Ask whether it reflects the assessed risk or the available budget, and whether governance over drawing it down is real.

Where are the interfaces, and who owns each one? Escalation loves the seams between packages, authorities and enabling works. Every interface without a clear owner is a cost event in waiting. Ask for the list, and ask who is accountable for each.

What has to go right on the schedule? A programme that only works if nothing slips is already over budget, it just does not know it yet. Ask what the critical path assumes, and what a realistic slip does to the cost of carry.

What would change the number, and when will we know? The useful question is not "is this the number" but "what are the three things that would move it, and at what point do we find out". That turns a single estimate into a set of tripwires the board can watch.

The point is not pessimism

None of this is about assuming the worst. Large projects can and do deliver to budget when the budget was built to be delivered to. The point is to know, before close, whether the number in front of you is a forecast or a hope, and to price the difference deliberately rather than discover it later.

An independent read at the right stage gate does exactly that. It does not slow the decision. It makes the decision an informed one, which on a programme of real scale is the only kind worth making.
