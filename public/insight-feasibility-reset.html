When a giga-programme needs a reset

By David Glossop · Gray Global

Every large programme carries a gap between what was announced and what can be built. On a well-run project that gap is small and closes early. On the ones that make headlines, it widens quietly through feasibility and design until the market, a lender, or a new chief executive forces it open all at once. By then the reset is expensive, public, and someone else's to manage.

The pattern is familiar across giga-scale development. A bold vision sets the scope. Early estimates are built to support the decision to proceed rather than to survive it. Optimism compounds through each stage, because every party has a reason to keep the number intact. Then reality arrives on site, and the programme is re-scoped, re-phased and re-budgeted under the worst possible conditions.

Scope that cannot be delivered is not ambition. It is unpriced risk sitting on someone's balance sheet.

Read the case, not the narrative

A feasibility case and the story told around it are two different documents. The narrative is about what the programme will become. The case is about what has actually been tested: the ground conditions, the enabling infrastructure, the approvals timeline, the market that has to absorb the output. An honest read starts by separating the two and asking which claims are evidenced and which are asserted.

The questions that matter are rarely the ones in the headline. Not "how large is the vision" but "which phase is fundable on its own, and does it stand up if the later phases never come". A programme that only works as a whole is a programme with no fallback. A programme designed so that an early phase delivers value independently can be reset without being abandoned.

Phasing is the discipline that saves the asset

The strongest thing an owner can do early is insist on deliverable, self-supporting phases. It is unglamorous work. It forces trade-offs that the original vision was built to avoid. But it converts an all-or-nothing bet into a sequence of decisions, each of which can be funded, tested and, if necessary, stopped without writing off everything before it.

This is where independent judgement pays for itself. The internal team is invested in the plan. The contractors are invested in the scope. An adviser who works only for the owner can say the uncomfortable thing while it is still cheap to hear: this phase is real, that one is aspiration, and the two should not share a budget line.

The reset you choose beats the one forced on you

Resets are not failures. The failure is leaving the reset until it is involuntary. A programme that re-phases deliberately, from a position of control, protects its credibility and its capital. One that waits until a lender, an auditor or the press forces the question pays for the delay in both.

If you are carrying a programme where the gap between the announcement and the evidence feels like it is widening, the right time to read it honestly was at the last stage gate. The second-best time is now, while the reset is still yours to design.
