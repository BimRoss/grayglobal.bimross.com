The owner-side gap

By David Glossop · Gray Global

Sit in enough project boardrooms and a pattern becomes clear. The contractor is advising on the contractor's scope. The designer is advising on the design. The financial adviser is advising toward the transaction that closes. Each is competent, and each is answering a slightly different question from the one the owner actually needs answered: should we proceed, on these terms, carrying this risk?

That is the owner-side gap. It is not caused by bad actors. It opens because every party around the table is doing their job, and no party's job is to hold the owner's position whole. The internal team is close to it and invested in it. The external advisers are paid against a scope that assumes the project goes ahead. The one perspective missing is the one with nothing to sell.

Independence is not a nicety. It is the only way to get advice that is indifferent to whether you proceed.

What the gap costs

The cost of the owner-side gap is rarely a single dramatic error. It is an accumulation of small, unchallenged assumptions. An interface everyone assumed someone else owned. A contingency sized to win approval rather than to cover the risk. A programme that works only if nothing slips. Each is defensible on its own. Together they are how a sound-looking case becomes an overrun.

The lenders learned this long ago, which is why serious project finance never closes without an independent technical adviser. The financiers do not take the borrower's word, the contractor's word, or their own enthusiasm as evidence. They buy an independent read. Owners deserve the same discipline on their own capital, and often have less of it.

Closing it

Closing the gap does not mean duplicating the team or second-guessing every decision. It means putting one independent party on the owner's side with a clear remit: challenge the assumptions that the invested parties cannot, test the claims that carry the most capital, and give the board a view it can act on without having to decode whose interest shaped it.

Done well, owner-side assurance is quiet. It surfaces the two or three things that actually move the decision, says them plainly, and gets out of the way. It is not an audit culture and it is not a brake on delivery. It is the difference between a board that believes its programme is sound and a board that knows why.

The economics are not close

An independent read is a small fraction of a major programme's cost, and it is spent before the largest commitments are made, when they can still be changed. The overrun it prevents, or the bad project it stops, is measured in a different order of magnitude. On the economics alone, the case for closing the owner-side gap is rarely the one that needs defending. The harder question is why so many programmes still proceed without it.
